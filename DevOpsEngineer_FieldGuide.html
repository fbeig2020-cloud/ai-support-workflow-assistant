DevOps Engineer Field Guide

Everything an AI Solution Architect needs from DevOps — not to build the pipeline yourself, but to direct the agents and engineers who do, evaluate what comes back, and know exactly where to plant your signature on the approval line.

How this guide works This is a knowledge base, not a slide deck. Use the search box at the top to jump straight to a term. Use Ask the guide (bottom right) to type a plain-language question — it answers from this document's own content, offline, with no external service involved. Use the left nav to browse by topic or to open one of the eight delivery documents, each of which is a real, downloadable artifact styled to Colaberry's document standard.

What "the 20%" means here

You will never personally write a Terraform module or tune a Kubernetes liveness probe. An AI coding agent, or an engineer working alongside one, will do that. Your job is to know enough of the discipline that you can:

  • Direct — write a directive precise enough that an agent cannot quietly skip the hard parts (rollback, secrets, idempotency, monitoring).
  • Evaluate — read a pipeline, a Terraform plan, or a runbook and tell working from merely plausible-looking.
  • Approve — sign off on a release with a specific number in mind (an SLO, an error budget, a rollback trigger) instead of a feeling.

The remainder of this guide teaches the concepts once, then applies every one of them to a single running example so the abstractions stay load-bearing rather than decorative.

Running example — used in every document below

CareQueue API — Meridian Health Network

One fictional service, carried through every diagram, runbook, and number in this guide, so that "the CI/CD pipeline" and "the SLO" always mean the same concrete thing.

The business situation

Meridian Health Network operates 12 outpatient clinics and a telehealth line across three states. Patients currently reach a human triage nurse by phone to be routed to the right department — a bottleneck during flu season and a source of missed-care complaints. Meridian is putting a new service into production to fix it.

CareQueue API is a real-time triage and routing microservice. A patient (or a nurse on their behalf) submits symptoms through the patient portal or the telehealth app; CareQueue scores urgency, checks clinician and department availability across the 12 clinics, and returns a routing decision — see a clinician now, book same-day, or route to telehealth — in under a second.

Why this is a good DevOps teaching case

  • Failure has a face. A bad deploy that misroutes an urgent case is not an abstract SLA breach — it is a patient safety incident. That raises the bar on rollback speed and canary discipline in a way a marketing site never would.
  • Regulated data. Symptom text and routing history touch PHI, which forces real answers on secrets management, environment parity, and who is allowed to see production data — not hand-waving.
  • Real traffic shape. Predictable daytime peaks, a flu-season spike, and a telehealth surge give the monitoring and SLO sections actual numbers to work with instead of placeholders.

Orientation

Why DevOps exists

DevOps is the answer to a specific, recurring failure: the team that builds software and the team that runs it had different incentives, different tools, and a wall between them.

The wall it replaced

Before DevOps, "Dev" was rewarded for shipping features and "Ops" was rewarded for nothing changing. Releases were rare, manual, and terrifying — a hand-off across a wall of tickets, with each side blaming the other when a 2 a.m. deploy went wrong. The fix was not a tool; it was collapsing the incentive split: the people who build a system also carry the pager for it, so the pressure to write reliable, observable, reversible code comes from inside the team, not from an external QA gate.

What changed in practice

  • Frequency over heroics. Small, frequent releases replace rare, big-bang ones — a mis-scored triage case reaches one canary cohort for ten minutes, not every patient for a week.
  • Automation over tribal knowledge. The deploy procedure lives in a pipeline definition and a runbook file, not in one engineer's memory.
  • Telemetry over guesswork. You find out CareQueue's p95 latency degraded from a dashboard and an alert, not from a clinician complaint three days later.
  • Reversibility over caution. Because rollback is fast and safe, teams can move fast on the way forward — the two are not in tension, they are the same investment.
Why this matters for you specifically As an AI Solution Architect, you inherit the DevOps mindset even though you will not carry the pager. Every directive you write for an agent should assume the agent's first draft is optimistic — no rollback path, no failure handling, secrets stapled into a config file. Your review lens is where the DevOps discipline actually gets enforced.
The 20% to know

CI/CD pipelines

Continuous Integration merges and validates code constantly; Continuous Delivery keeps it always in a releasable state; Continuous Deployment pushes every passing change to production automatically. CareQueue uses CI + Delivery, with a human approval gate before the production canary — appropriate given the patient-safety blast radius.

What a pipeline actually is

A pipeline is a chain of gates, not a single script. Each stage exists to catch a specific class of failure cheaply, before it reaches a stage where it is expensive: a syntax error is cheap to catch at build; a broken triage score is cheap to catch at unit test; a bad interaction between CareQueue and Postgres is cheap to catch at integration test; and a genuinely surprising production behavior is cheapest to catch at a 10% canary instead of 100% of traffic.

What good looks like

  • Every stage is automated and reversible — no stage where a human types a command from memory.
  • One build artifact (one container image, one tag) is promoted across every environment. Nothing is rebuilt for staging vs. prod.
  • Gates fail closed: if the security scan can't run, the pipeline stops — it does not skip to deploy.
  • Rollback is a pipeline action, not a separate manual runbook improvised under pressure.
Reviewer's trap An agent-generated pipeline that "looks complete" but rebuilds the image separately per environment has silently broken the one-artifact rule — staging and prod are no longer guaranteed to run the same bits. See the full CI/CD Pipeline Design for the CareQueue implementation.

Build, release, run separation

Three distinct stages that must never collapse into one step, borrowed from the twelve-factor app methodology.

StageWhat happensCareQueue example
BuildSource is compiled into an executable artifactdocker build produces careq-api:sha-8f2c1a, pushed to ECR once
ReleaseA build is combined with environment-specific configThe image tag is bound to staging's or prod's Helm values.yaml (DB endpoint, feature flags, replica count) — never rebuilt
RunThe release executes in an environmentKubernetes schedules the pods; the running process holds no state that build/release don't already know about
Why it matters If a bug appears only in prod, this separation tells you where to look: is it the artifact (same everywhere — a real code bug) or the release (environment-specific config — check secrets, flags, resource limits)? Collapsing the stages destroys that diagnostic power.
The 20% to know

Infrastructure as Code (IaC)

Infrastructure — networks, clusters, databases, IAM roles — is defined in version-controlled declarative files (Terraform, in CareQueue's case), not clicked together in a console.

  • Reviewable. A VPC change goes through the same pull-request review as application code.
  • Reproducible. Standing up a fourth environment is terraform apply with a new variable file, not a week of console archaeology.
  • Diffable. terraform plan shows exactly what will change before it changes — the architect's approval point.
  • Drift-detectable. If someone hand-edits a security group in the console, the next plan shows the drift instead of hiding it.
Non-negotiable Secrets never live in Terraform state or variable files in plaintext. CareQueue pulls DB credentials and API keys from AWS Secrets Manager at deploy time — see the full Infrastructure-as-Code Plan.
The 20% to know

Environments and parity

Dev, staging, and production should differ only in scale and data — never in kind. "It worked in staging" should mean something.

  • Same artifact everywhere. The container image promoted to prod is byte-identical to what passed staging.
  • Same topology, smaller scale. Staging runs the same Helm chart as prod with fewer replicas and smaller instance types — not a different architecture.
  • Data parity without data risk. Staging uses de-identified, synthetic-shaped data at realistic volume; only prod touches real PHI.
  • Config parity, value difference. The same environment variables exist in every tier; only their values differ, and they come from the environment, never from hardcoded branches in code.

Full detail, including the environment matrix (downloadable as CSV), lives in the Environment Strategy document.

The 20% to know

Containers

A container packages CareQueue's code, runtime, and dependencies into one immutable, portable unit — the thing that actually moves through build → release → run.

  • Immutability. Once built, an image is never patched in place; a fix means a new image with a new tag.
  • Small surface. CareQueue's runtime image is a distroless final layer — no shell, no package manager, minimal CVE surface.
  • Orchestration does the rest. Kubernetes (EKS) handles scheduling, restarts, scaling, and health-check-driven traffic routing so the container itself stays dumb and disposable.
Review question "Can this container be killed and replaced right now with zero data loss?" If the honest answer is no, state is leaking into the container that belongs in Postgres or Redis instead.

The 20% to know

Rollback

A rollback is only as good as the plan written before you needed it. It has three parts, and an agent will happily skip two of them if not told otherwise.

  • Trigger — a specific, monitored condition (error rate, latency, failed health check) that starts the rollback, ideally automatically.
  • Target — the exact previous version to return to, known in advance (the last known-good tag), not "whatever was there before."
  • Data safety — the hard part. Code can roll back instantly; a database migration that dropped a column cannot. CareQueue enforces the expand-contract pattern so every migration is backward-compatible with the prior code version.
The question that catches bad plans "If we roll back the code, will the currently-deployed database schema still work with the old version?" If the answer is no, the release is not actually rollback-safe — see the full Rollback Plan.
The 20% to know

Monitoring, logging, and alerting

Three different jobs, often confused. Monitoring tells you something is wrong. Logging tells you why. Alerting tells the right human, fast enough to matter.

  • Monitoring / metrics — numeric time series: request rate, error rate, latency, queue depth. Cheap to query, good for dashboards and alert thresholds.
  • Logging — structured events with context, used to diagnose a specific incident after an alert fires. CareQueue logs JSON with a correlation ID that traces one patient submission end-to-end.
  • Alerting — a rule that turns a metric threshold into a page. Alert on symptoms the patient or business feels (elevated error rate, slow triage response), not on causes (CPU is at 80%) — causes are for the dashboard you check after the page.
Reviewer's trap A pipeline or service with no alert owner is a service nobody actually watches. Every row in the Alert Catalog names a person or on-call rotation, never just a channel.
The 20% to know

SLIs, SLOs, and error budgets

The vocabulary that turns "make it reliable" into a number you can hold an agent — or a vendor — accountable to.

  • SLI (Service Level Indicator) — a measured quantity, e.g. "% of triage requests answered under 800ms."
  • SLO (Service Level Objective) — the target for that indicator, e.g. "99.9% of requests, measured monthly." Never promise 100% — it is unachievable and it deletes the ability to take any risk at all.
  • Error budget — the inverse of the SLO: 0.1% of a month is roughly 43 minutes CareQueue is allowed to be unavailable. That budget is a resource the team spends deliberately — a risky release, a chaos-test — not an accident to be ashamed of.
  • Burn rate — how fast the budget is being consumed. A fast burn (would exhaust the month's budget in hours) pages someone now; a slow burn creates a ticket.

Real numbers, real burn-rate alert thresholds, and the budget policy live in the SLO / Error-Budget Sheet.

The 20% to know

Incident response and runbooks

A runbook is a rehearsed procedure, written before the incident, so that 2 a.m. decision-making is reduced to "follow step 4."

  • Detect — an alert fires against a named SLI.
  • Triage — an on-call engineer confirms severity and opens an incident channel.
  • Mitigate — the fastest safe action, usually rollback, not a forward fix under pressure.
  • Resolve & communicate — status updates on a fixed cadence to stakeholders (for CareQueue: clinical operations lead is notified within 15 minutes of a Sev-1).
  • Postmortem — blameless, written within 48 hours, produces action items with owners — not just "be more careful."

The step-by-step release procedure for CareQueue is the Deployment Runbook; the failure-path counterpart is the Rollback Plan.

Architect's judgment

The architect's review lens

A fast checklist for reading any AI-generated pipeline, IaC plan, or runbook before you sign off on it.

  1. One artifact, many environments? If the pipeline rebuilds per environment, reject it.
  2. Where are the secrets? Grep the diff for anything that looks like a key, token, or connection string. If found, reject.
  3. What is the rollback trigger, in numbers? "We'll monitor it" is not a trigger. An error-rate percentage and a time window are.
  4. Does a migration in this release break the previous version? If yes, it must ship as two releases (expand, then contract), not one.
  5. Who does the alert page, by name or rotation? A Slack channel with no owner is not an alert route.
  6. Is the SLO a real number below 100%? If it says "100% uptime," send it back.
  7. Can I find the four failure-first answers? What happens if this fails, does it retry, what's the recovery path, and what failure modes are explicitly out of scope.

1. Purpose & scope

This document defines the automated pipeline that takes CareQueue API source code from a merged change on main to production traffic. It covers build, test, security, packaging, promotion, progressive deployment, and rollback. It applies to the careq-api repository only; shared platform pipelines are documented separately.

2. Design principles

  • One immutable artifact is built once and promoted unchanged through every environment.
  • Every gate fails closed — a gate that cannot run stops the pipeline rather than being skipped.
  • No manual-only deploy path exists to production; the pipeline is the only route.
  • Rollback is automatic on failed canary analysis, not dependent on a human noticing first.
  • Secrets are injected at deploy time from AWS Secrets Manager; none are present in the repository, pipeline logs, or container image.

4. Rollback within the pipeline

Rollback is a first-class pipeline action, not a separate manual procedure. Argo Rollouts holds the previous ReplicaSet warm during the entire canary sequence specifically so reverting is a traffic-weight change, not a redeploy. Full trigger conditions, target-version selection, and data-safety rules are in the Rollback Plan.

5. Failure-first answers

QuestionAnswer
What happens if this fails?The pipeline halts at the failing gate; nothing downstream executes; the failing stage is visible in the PR status checks.
Will it retry?Only the ECR push retries (2 attempts, exponential backoff). All test and analysis gates are non-retrying by design — a flaky-looking failure must be diagnosed, not silently re-run.
Recovery path if retries exhausted?Pipeline run is marked failed; engineer fixes and re-pushes. No dead-letter queue applies to pipeline runs — the source of truth is the failing commit.
Failure modes explicitly out of scopeSimultaneous multi-region AWS outage during a deploy window; concurrent pipeline runs against the same commit (blocked by GitHub Actions concurrency groups, not handled by this document).

1. Scope

All CareQueue cloud infrastructure — networking, the EKS cluster, datastores, IAM, and secrets scaffolding — is defined in Terraform under infra/careq/. Nothing in this footprint is created or modified through the AWS console outside of a documented break-glass emergency, which requires a same-day retroactive Terraform import.

3. State management

  • Remote state in a dedicated S3 bucket (meridian-tfstate-careq), versioned, with server-side encryption (SSE-KMS).
  • State locking via a DynamoDB table (tfstate-locks) — prevents two engineers or two pipeline runs from applying concurrently.
  • Separate state files per environment (dev, staging, prod) using Terraform workspaces; prod state additionally requires MFA-protected role assumption to apply.

4. Change flow

  1. Engineer or agent opens a PR against infra/careq/.
  2. CI runs terraform fmt -check, terraform validate, and terraform plan, posting the plan output as a PR comment.
  3. A human (DevOps Engineering) reviews the plan diff — this is the architect's approval point for infrastructure change.
  4. On merge, terraform apply runs automatically for dev and staging; production apply requires a second manual approval.
  5. Nightly drift-detection job runs terraform plan against all three environments and posts any unexpected diff to the DevOps Slack channel.

5. Secrets management

Non-negotiable No secret value is ever written to a .tf, .tfvars, or state file in plaintext. Terraform provisions the Secrets Manager entry and its rotation policy; the actual DB password and API keys are generated at first-boot by the rotation Lambda and read by the application via the AWS SDK at runtime, never baked into the image or Helm values.

Parity rules across local, dev, staging, and production, and the promotion path a single build takes through them.

1. Parity principle

Every CareQueue environment runs the exact same container image and the exact same Helm chart version. The only permitted differences are scale (replica count, instance size), data (synthetic vs. de-identified vs. real), and configuration values (never configuration structure). If a bug is environment-specific, it must trace to one of those three axes — never to different code.

All environment-specific values are injected via Kubernetes ConfigMaps and Secrets at deploy time, sourced from per-environment Helm values-{env}.yaml files and Secrets Manager. Deployment Runbook — CareQueue API

Followed by the on-call release captain for every production release. Written so a junior engineer can execute it without improvising.
Version1.3
OwnerSRE On-Call Rotation
StatusApproved
Date2026-08-28
ClassificationInternal

1. Pre-conditions

  • PR has passed all CI/CD gates through the manual approval stage (see CI/CD Pipeline Design).
  • Release window is open — CareQueue releases are avoided during clinic peak hours (Mon–Fri 8–10am and 4–6pm local) unless the change is a Sev-1 hotfix.
  • Release captain has confirmed the current error-budget status is healthy (see SLO / Error-Budget Sheet) — do not ship a risky release against an exhausted budget.
Freeze condition If the monthly error budget has under 20% remaining, only Sev-1 fixes may deploy; all other releases wait for budget reset or an explicit VP Engineering override.

2. Step-by-step procedure

  1. Announce. Post in #careq-releases: version tag, change summary, rollback tag, expected canary duration (~30 min end to end).
  2. Confirm staging health. Check the Grafana "CareQueue — Staging" dashboard for green SLIs over the last hour.
  3. Trigger canary. In the CI/CD pipeline UI, click Promote to Production on the approved run. This applies the Argo Rollout at 10% traffic automatically.
  4. Watch the 10% window (10 min). Monitor the "CareQueue — Canary Analysis" Grafana panel: error rate, p95 latency, pod restarts. Do not proceed manually — Argo Rollouts' automated analysis gates the next stage.
  5. Confirm auto-promotion to 50%. Verify in the Argo Rollouts UI that the rollout advanced; watch the same panel for another 10-minute window.
  6. Confirm auto-promotion to 100%. Verify final rollout status is Healthy and traffic weight is 100% on the new ReplicaSet.
  7. Run the post-deploy smoke test. Pipeline auto-fires a synthetic triage request; confirm it returns a valid routing decision in the pipeline logs.
  8. Close the loop. Post in #careq-releases: "Release complete, 100% traffic, SLIs nominal." Update the release log.

3. Verification checklist

CheckExpected result
Error rate (5 min rolling)< 1%
p95 triage-scoring latency< 800ms
Pod restart count since deploy0
Synthetic smoke requestReturns HTTP 200 with a valid routing payload
Clinician queue dashboardLoads and reflects the new routing decisions in real time

4. When to abort

If any verification check fails during the canary window, do not wait for automated rollback to confirm — trigger it immediately: see the Rollback Plan for the exact command and communication steps.

5. Failure-first answers

QuestionAnswer
What happens if the release captain is unreachable mid-canary?Argo Rollouts' automated analysis continues independent of any human — a bad canary rolls back with no human action required.
Will it retry?The pipeline promotion step itself is not retried automatically; the captain re-triggers manually after confirming root cause.
Recovery path?Escalate to SRE on-call via PagerDuty if the release captain cannot resolve within 15 minutes of an abort.
Out of scopeCoordinated multi-service releases (CareQueue ships independently; cross-service release trains are not yet defined).

Rollback Plan — CareQueue API

What triggers a rollback, exactly which version it targets, and how patient data stays safe through the reversal.

1. Rollback triggers

TriggerThresholdMechanism
Error rate> 2% of requests over 5 minutesAutomatic — Argo Rollouts analysis template
Latencyp95 > 1.5s over 5 minutesAutomatic — Argo Rollouts analysis template
Failed health checksAny pod failing readiness 3× consecutivelyAutomatic — Kubernetes + Argo Rollouts
Manual triggerOn-call judgment — e.g. a correctness bug not caught by the above (a routing decision is medically wrong but fast and low-error-rate)Manual — release captain or SRE on-call runs the rollback command

2. Target version selection

The rollback target is always the immediately preceding stable tag — the ReplicaSet Argo Rollouts kept warm through the canary window, identified by its git SHA. The on-call engineer never guesses; the target is read directly from the Rollout object's status.stableRS field, which is the single source of truth.

# Identify the current stable ReplicaSet and roll back to it
kubectl argo rollouts get rollout careq-api -n careq-prod
kubectl argo rollouts undo careq-api -n careq-prod
kubectl argo rollouts status careq-api -n careq-prod --watch

3. Data safety

Rolling back code is instant; rolling back a database schema is not always possible. CareQueue enforces the expand-contract migration pattern specifically so that every code rollback is safe against the currently-deployed schema:

  • Expand: a migration adds a new column/table without removing the old one. This ships and is fully deployed before any code that depends on it.
  • Migrate: the new code version reads/writes the new structure while remaining tolerant of the old one during the transition window.
  • Contract: only once the new code has been stable in production for a full release cycle does a separate, later migration remove the old structure.
Rule No single release both changes application behavior and performs a destructive schema change (drop column, drop table, non-nullable column with no default). If a directive to an agent implies both in one release, split it into two releases before it ships.

4. Rollback procedure

  1. Confirm the trigger condition against the Grafana "CareQueue — Canary Analysis" dashboard (or accept the automatic trigger already firing).
  2. If manual: run the kubectl argo rollouts undo command above.
  3. Confirm traffic weight returns to 100% on the prior stable ReplicaSet within 5 minutes.
  4. Post in #careq-releases and page the on-call SRE lead if not already engaged.
  5. Open an incident ticket; freeze further CareQueue releases until root cause is identified.
  6. Within 48 hours, complete a blameless postmortem with action items and owners.

5. Failure-first answers

QuestionAnswer
What happens if this fails?If rollouts undo does not converge within 5 minutes, the on-call engineer manually scales the prior ReplicaSet directly via kubectl scale as a break-glass fallback.
Will it retry?The undo command is safe to re-run — it is idempotent against the current Rollout state.
Recovery path if all else fails?Declare a Sev-1, engage VP Engineering, and consider taking CareQueue out of the routing path entirely (fail open to the legacy phone-triage queue) while the platform is stabilized.
Out of scopePoint-in-time database restore for data corruption (not a code-rollback scenario — covered separately by Meridian's RDS backup/restore procedure).

4. Error-budget policy

  • > 50% remaining: normal release cadence, including experimental features behind flags.
  • 20–50% remaining: proceed, but the release captain flags any release that trades reliability for speed (e.g. skipping the 50% canary stage) for SRE lead sign-off.
  • < 20% remaining: freeze all non-Sev-1 releases; engineering time shifts to reliability work until the budget recovers or the month resets.
  • Budget exhausted: production changes require VP Engineering approval regardless of severity, except an active Sev-1 fix.
Why 99.9%, not 100% A 100% SLO is unfalsifiable and removes the ability to take any deliberate risk — including the canary releases that make the pipeline safer overall. 99.9% is a real, defensible number Meridian's clinical operations team has agreed matches patient-safety expectations for a routing aid that still has a human fallback.

5. Failure-first answers

QuestionAnswer
What happens if the budget is exhausted mid-incident?The incident response continues regardless — the policy governs new releases, not active mitigation.
Will it retry?Not applicable — budget tracking is a continuous rolling calculation, not a retryable operation.
Recovery path?Budget resets on a rolling 30-day window, so sustained good behavior recovers headroom day by day rather than waiting for a calendar reset.
Out of scopeMulti-service, org-wide error-budget aggregation — each Meridian service tracks its own budget independently.
