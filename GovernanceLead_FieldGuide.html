Governance Lead Field Guide

Everything an AI Solution Architect needs from AI governance — not to write every control yourself, but to direct the agents and teams who build them, evaluate what comes back, and know exactly where your signature belongs on the approval line.

How this guide works This is a knowledge base, not a slide deck. Use the search box at the top to jump straight to a term. Use Ask the guide (bottom right) to type a plain-language question — it answers from this document's own content, offline, with no external service involved. Use the left nav to browse by topic or to open one of the seven governance documents, each a real, downloadable artifact styled to Colaberry's document standard.

What "the 20%" means here

You will never personally write a bias-testing script or draft a regulatory filing. A model risk analyst, a compliance officer, or an AI agent working under your direction will do that. Your job is to know enough of the discipline that you can:

  • Direct — write a governance directive precise enough that a team (or an agent) cannot quietly ship an AI system with no owner, no risk appetite, and no kill-switch.
  • Evaluate — read a RACI chart, a risk register, or a compliance checklist and tell a control that actually works from one that merely looks tidy on a slide.
  • Approve — sign off on a deployment, a retrain, or an override policy with a specific number in mind (a risk appetite threshold, a bias-test ratio, an evidence artifact) instead of a feeling.

The remainder of this guide teaches the concepts once, then applies every one of them to a single running example so the abstractions stay load-bearing rather than decorative.

Running example — used in every document below

ACTA — the Aegis Auto Claims Triage Assistant

One fictional AI initiative, carried through every framework, register, and threshold in this guide, so that "the risk appetite" and "the human-oversight trigger" always mean the same concrete thing.

The business situation

Aegis Mutual is a mid-size auto insurer handling roughly 4,200 new auto claims per week. Historically, every First Notice of Loss (FNOL) waited in a single queue for a human adjuster to read, classify, and route — an average wait of 19 hours that drove customer complaints and adjuster burnout during storm season.

ACTA is the AI assistant Aegis Mutual's leadership approved to fix that. The moment an FNOL arrives, ACTA reads it and produces four outputs: a severity classification, a fraud-risk score (from the third-party vendor SentinelScore, 0–100), a routing decision (Fast-Track auto-approval, standard Adjuster Review, or referral to the Special Investigations Unit — SIU), and an initial reserve estimate. A human adjuster always retains override authority; ACTA never closes a claim on its own — it is a decision-support tool, not an autonomous decision-maker. That single sentence is the fact this entire governance program exists to enforce, verify, and prove — to the Board, to regulators, and to claimants — every single day.

Why this is a good governance teaching case

  • Consequential decisions. A wrong fraud flag on an honest claimant, or an unreviewed denial, is not an abstract model-accuracy statistic — it is a person's car, a person's medical bill, and in some states a bad-faim-claims-handling lawsuit. That raises the bar on human oversight and explainability far above a typical recommendation engine.
  • Regulated industry, multiple frameworks. Insurance is state-regulated (unfair claims practices acts, several states' emerging AI-in-insurance bulletins modeled on the NAIC's Model Bulletin on the Use of AI Systems), the carrier is SOC 2- and ISO 27001-aligned for its technology controls, and it has voluntarily adopted GDPR-style automated-decision safeguards as its internal privacy bar — giving the compliance mapping real teeth instead of a single narrow framework.
  • A real, current AI system with real vendors. ACTA sits on top of six systems, two of them third-party AI vendors (SentinelScore for fraud scoring, DocScan AI for document OCR) that Aegis Mutual does not control — which is exactly the seam where AI governance earns its keep: you cannot govern what a vendor does internally, only what you require of the interface, the evidence, and the fallback.

The two numbers every document in this guide agrees on

The governing thresholds Fast-Track auto-approval requires ALL THREE: model confidence ≥ 0.90, reserve estimate < $25,000, and a SentinelScore fraud score in the LOW band (< 40 of 100). Any fraud score ≥ 70 (HIGH or CRITICAL band) is routed to SIU for mandatory human review — never Fast-Tracked, regardless of confidence or reserve size. Every RACI decision, risk-appetite threshold, and human-oversight trigger in this guide is written against these two numbers, not invented fresh per document.
Orientation

Why governance exists — and how it differs from management

Management makes ACTA work. Governance proves ACTA is allowed to keep working — to the Board, to regulators, and to the claimant on the other end of a denial letter.

The distinction that trips up new architects

Management asks "is it running, is it fast enough, did the sprint ship." It is owned by the people who build and operate ACTA day to day — Claims Operations and ML Engineering. Governance asks a different question entirely: "who is allowed to decide this, what evidence proves the decision was sound, and what happens when it goes wrong." Governance does not build the model faster; it makes sure that when someone — an adjuster, a regulator, a plaintiff's attorney, the Board — asks "who approved this and why," there is a specific, documented, single-throated answer, not a shrug.

A team can be excellent at management (ACTA has 99.7% uptime, adjusters love the console) and still have zero governance (nobody can produce the bias test that proves the fraud score doesn't disproportionately flag one ZIP code, and nobody actually knows who is accountable for approving a threshold change). That gap is invisible until the day it is very visible — an audit, an incident, a regulator's letter — and by then it is a Board-level problem, not an engineering one.

What governance is not

  • Not a document that exists to satisfy an auditor once a year — it is a set of controls that run continuously and produce evidence as a byproduct of running.
  • Not a veto held by one risk-averse office — decision rights are distributed by decision type (see RACI), not concentrated in a single gatekeeper who becomes a bottleneck.
  • Not a slower version of management — a well-run governance program makes deployment faster over time, because the deployment checklist is known in advance instead of negotiated ad hoc under deadline pressure.
The question that separates the two disciplines Ask a manager "does it work?" Ask a governance lead "if it stopped working safely tomorrow, who would know first, who is accountable for the fix, and what is the evidence that the fix actually worked?" If the second question has no clean answer, you do not have governance — you have hope with a dashboard.

The 20% to know

Decision rights and RACI

Governance fails first at the boundary between "a lot of people care about this" and "exactly one person is accountable for it." RACI is the tool that forces that boundary into the open.

  • Responsible — does the work to produce the decision or artifact. Can be more than one role.
  • Accountable — owns the outcome and answers for it. Exactly one per decision — the single most important rule in this whole discipline, because "the team decided" is how bad decisions survive their own postmortem.
  • Consulted — has relevant input sought before the decision is made (two-way conversation).
  • Informed — told after the decision is made (one-way notice).
The trap an AI agent will walk into Ask an AI coding agent to "add an approval step" and it will often generate a workflow where a Slack channel is "responsible" for approval — nobody is accountable, so a bad deploy has no name attached to it. Every RACI row in the Decision RACI names an individual role, never a channel, a committee-in-the-abstract, or "the team."

ACTA's RACI covers ten decisions, from initial production deployment down to a single-case human override — see the full Decision RACI for the complete matrix with exactly one Accountable role per row.

The 20% to know

Policies, standards, and controls — and how they chain together

These three words get used interchangeably in casual conversation and that is exactly how governance programs end up with policies nobody can point to a control for.

TermWhat it isACTA example
PolicyA statement of intent and rules — what must be true, and why"No claimant is denied without a documented human sign-off" (Responsible-AI Policy)
StandardThe specific, measurable bar a policy is held to"Sign-off must be logged within the same case record, by a named adjuster, before NotifyHub sends the notice"
ControlThe mechanism — technical or procedural — that makes the standard actually happen, and that produces evidence that it happenedThe claim-status state machine will not transition to "denied-communicated" without a non-null sign_off_by field; NotifyHub's send API rejects the call otherwise
The chain, in one line Policy says what must be true. Standard says how you'd measure it. Control is the thing that actually stops the bad path and leaves a trace. A policy with no traceable control is a wish, not governance — see the Compliance Checklist's Evidence column for what "traceable" looks like in practice.
The 20% to know

The risk register and risk appetite

A risk register that never assigns a number is a list of worries. Risk appetite is the number that turns "we're aware of this" into "here is the threshold at which we stop and act."

  • Likelihood × Impact — each risk is scored on two 1–5 scales; the product (1–25) is the risk score, plotted on a 5×5 heatmap.
  • Inherent vs. residual — inherent is the risk before any mitigation exists; residual is what's left after the mitigation is actually running. The gap between the two is the mitigation's entire justification for existing.
  • Risk appetite — the Board-approved line: how much residual risk the organization will tolerate before it must escalate. Aegis Mutual's Board has set ACTA's appetite at three tiers: ≤8 Within Appetite (standing monitoring only), 9–14 Accepted with Monitoring (requires CRO sign-off and quarterly review), ≥15 Exceeds Appetite (immediate mitigation plan and Board Risk Committee notification within 5 business days).
Reviewer's trap A risk register where every single residual score is 1–4 is not a well-governed system — it's a register nobody stress-tested. See the full Risk Register: several inherent scores reach 20 (algorithmic bias, inadequate human oversight) and one residual — vendor concentration on SentinelScore — is deliberately left at "Accepted with Monitoring," not forced down to zero, because a single-source AI vendor is a risk you manage, not one you can fully eliminate without rebuilding the fraud model in-house.
The 20% to know

AI-specific risk: the categories a generic risk register misses

A traditional IT risk register covers security and availability well. AI systems add categories that don't map cleanly onto either — miss them and your register will look complete while missing the risks that actually cause incidents.

CategoryWhat it isACTA instance
Bias / fairnessThe model's outputs correlate with a protected class, even without using the protected attribute directly (proxy discrimination)SentinelScore's fraud referral rate disparately flagging claimants by ZIP code as a proxy for race or income
PrivacyPersonal or sensitive data is collected, retained, or exposed beyond what's needed or disclosedBodily-injury claim attachments carrying medical detail beyond what triage requires
SecurityThe model, its data, or its infrastructure is a new attack surface (prompt/data poisoning, model theft, adversarial inputs)An adjuster or claimant crafting an FNOL narrative specifically to game the fraud score downward
Model driftReal-world data shifts away from what the model was trained/validated on, silently degrading accuracyClaim patterns after a hurricane look nothing like the training distribution — false negatives spike
MisuseThe system is used outside its approved scope or by people who shouldn't have access to override itACTA used to triage a claim type or state it was never validated for; an adjuster's override rate suggesting rubber-stamping rather than genuine review

Every one of these five categories has at least one corresponding row in the Risk Register and at least one human-oversight trigger in the Responsible-AI Policy — a category with no register entry and no trigger is a category that isn't actually governed yet, whatever the slide deck claims.

The 20% to know

Compliance mapping: SOC 2, ISO 27001, and GDPR, at the concept level

You do not need to be a certified auditor for any of these three frameworks. You need to know what each one is actually checking for, so you can tell a control that satisfies it from one that only sounds like it does.

FrameworkWhat it actually checksWhy it applies to ACTA
SOC 2Five Trust Service Criteria — Security, Availability, Processing Integrity, Confidentiality, Privacy — verified by an independent auditor against controls you define and evidence you produceAegis Mutual's technology stack (including ACTA) is SOC 2 Type II audited annually; claims data confidentiality and processing-integrity (model output accuracy) are the two criteria examiners scrutinize hardest for an AI system
ISO 27001An information security management system standard; Annex A lists control domains (access control, supplier relationships, incident management, etc.) an organization must address and evidenceAegis Mutual's ISO 27001 certification scope explicitly includes the AI system inventory (ACTA, SentinelScore, DocScan) as in-scope assets requiring documented risk treatment
GDPR (voluntarily adopted, concept level)EU privacy regulation whose automated-decision-making safeguard (Art. 22 — no purely automated decision with legal/significant effect without meaningful human involvement) Aegis Mutual has adopted as an internal best-practice bar, alongside its reinsurance partners' EU data-sharing requirementsDirectly maps onto ACTA's core design constraint: no claim denial is ever communicated without a human sign-off — this is GDPR Art. 22 compliance by construction, not an afterthought
The concept that ties all three together Every framework above is really asking the same underlying question in different vocabulary: "can you prove, after the fact, that the control you claim exists actually ran?" The Compliance Checklist's Evidence column exists precisely because "we have a policy for that" is not an answer any of the three frameworks accepts.
The 20% to know

Audit trails and evidence

An audit trail that can be edited after the fact is not an audit trail — it's a diary the defendant wrote. Two properties separate real evidence from a log file.

  • Immutable / tamper-evident — every override, sign-off, threshold change, and denial notice is written once and hash-chained, so editing or deleting a past entry breaks the chain for every entry after it, visibly.
  • Attributable — every entry names a specific person or system identity, a timestamp, and the decision made — never an anonymous "system updated record."
Reviewer's trap "We log everything" is not evidence of governance — retrievability is. If Internal Audit cannot pull, in under an hour, every denial ACTA recommended in the last quarter along with the adjuster's sign-off for each one, the log exists but the control doesn't. See the Audit Plan's AP-08 test, which recomputes the hash chain on a sampled period specifically to catch a log that "exists" but has quietly been edited.
The 20% to know

Responsible AI and human oversight, with a number attached

"A human is in the loop" is not a control until it names the exact condition that pulls the human in, and the exact consequence if that condition is ignored.

A human-oversight trigger has three required parts: a numeric condition (not "when it seems risky"), a named role that must act, and a hard stop if the role doesn't act — the system does not proceed on its own by default. ACTA's four governing triggers:

TriggerConditionRequired action
Fraud referralSentinelScore score ≥ 70 (HIGH/CRITICAL band)Mandatory SIU analyst review before any routing decision proceeds — never Fast-Tracked
Denial sign-offAny claim denial recommendation, regardless of amountMandatory second-line (Compliance & Legal-reviewed template) sign-off before NotifyHub sends the notice
Large reserveReserve or payout ≥ $50,000Senior Claims Manager approval in addition to standard Adjuster Review
Override-pattern anomalyA single adjuster's override rate exceeds 30% in a rolling 30-day windowAutomatic compliance-audit trigger and Model Risk Management review of that adjuster's queue

The complete acceptable-use rules, prohibited uses, and escalation path live in the Responsible-AI Policy.

The 20% to know

Operating model and cadence

Governance that only convenes when something breaks is not governance — it's incident response wearing a governance hat. A real operating model has standing bodies that meet whether or not anything is on fire.

  • Standing bodies — named, chartered groups with defined membership and authority, not an ad hoc meeting invite.
  • Cadence — a fixed meeting rhythm (weekly, bi-weekly, monthly, quarterly) so oversight happens on a schedule, not when someone remembers.
  • Escalation path — a defined route from the working level up to the Board when a risk or decision exceeds the authority of the level below it.

Aegis Mutual runs four standing bodies for ACTA, from a weekly working group up to a quarterly Board Risk Committee review — see the full Operating Model & Cadence.

Good vs. bad governance

Both a well-governed and a poorly-governed AI system can pass a demo and even a first audit. The difference shows up the first time something goes wrong, or the first time someone asks a pointed question.

SignalBad governanceGood governance
Accountability"The team" or "the AI Governance Council" approved it — no individual nameExactly one named Accountable role per decision, traceable in the RACI
Risk appetite"We try to keep risk low" — no numberBoard-approved numeric thresholds (≤8 / 9–14 / ≥15) with defined actions at each
Human oversight"A human is in the loop" with no defined triggerA numeric condition (score ≥ 70, amount ≥ $50,000) tied to a named role and a hard stop
Evidence"We log everything" — logs exist but were never retrieved or testedQuarterly control tests that actually pull the evidence and verify it, with a sign-off trail
Bias testingTested once before launch, never againRecurring (quarterly) disparate-impact testing with a defined pass/fail ratio (four-fifths rule)
Vendor riskVendor contract signed, no ongoing reassessmentAnnual vendor risk reassessment plus a documented manual-fallback procedure if the vendor fails
Incident responseNo kill-switch, or a kill-switch nobody has ever actually testedA named authority (CRO) who can suspend the system, rehearsed via a tabletop exercise

The one-line test Good governance survives a subpoena. If every row above only holds up when nobody asks a hard question, it isn't governance — it's a policy binder.

Architect's judgment

The architect's review lens

Seven checks to run, in order, on any AI-generated governance artifact before you approve it. If any of these fail, send it back — don't fix it yourself and don't approve it "with a note."

  1. Exactly one Accountable per decision. If a RACI row lists two Accountables, or a committee instead of a role, it's not done.
  2. Every risk has a number, not an adjective. "High risk" fails; "Likelihood 4, Impact 5, inherent score 20" passes.
  3. Every human-oversight trigger has a threshold and a hard stop. "Reviewed as needed" fails; "score ≥ 70, mandatory SIU review, cannot proceed without it" passes.
  4. Every compliance obligation has an evidence column entry, not just a control name. "We have access controls" fails; "quarterly access review report, attached" passes.
  5. Residual risk is shown, not just inherent. A register with only inherent scores hasn't proven the mitigation does anything.
  6. The document names who tests the control, and when. A control with no test schedule in the Audit Plan is a control that runs on faith.
  7. Nothing reads as "everything is fine." A risk register with every score in the green, or a compliance checklist with every row "Implemented," is a document that wasn't stress-tested — real programs carry open items and monitored (not eliminated) risks.
The single fastest tell Open the Risk Register first. If nothing on it is inherently scored 15 or higher, either ACTA is a far simpler system than described, or the register was written to look clean rather than to be useful. A governance document with no uncomfortable numbers in it has not done its job.

1. Purpose

This Framework establishes how Aegis Mutual governs ACTA across its entire lifecycle — design, validation, deployment, operation, retraining, and eventual retirement. It exists so that "who allowed this, and on what basis" always has a documented, single-throated answer, independent of who happens to be in the room when a question is asked.

2. Scope

This Framework applies to ACTA's classification, fraud-scoring integration (SentinelScore), routing, and reserve-estimation functions, and to every system ACTA depends on for a governed decision: the Policy Admin System (policy truth), DocScan AI (document extraction), ClaimPay (payment execution), and NotifyHub (claimant communication). It does not govern Aegis Mutual's non-AI claims systems, which fall under the standard IT Risk Framework.

3. Governing principles

  • Human accountability is never delegated to the model. ACTA recommends; a named human decides, on every claim it touches, at the point specified by the Responsible-AI Policy.
  • Every decision has exactly one Accountable role. No decision in ACTA's lifecycle is accountable to "the team" or "the committee" — see the Decision RACI.
  • Risk is quantified, not described. Every material risk carries a likelihood, an impact, and a residual score measured against the Board-approved appetite.
  • Every obligation has evidence. A control that cannot produce evidence on demand is treated as not existing, regardless of intent.
  • Independence is structural, not aspirational. The team that builds and operates ACTA (1st line) is never the same team that validates or audits it (2nd and 3rd lines).

4. Control structure — three lines of defense

Aegis Mutual governs ACTA using the standard three-lines-of-defense model, adapted for an AI system. The first line owns and operates; the second line independently validates and challenges; the third line provides independent assurance directly to the Board, bypassing the reporting chain of the first two lines entirely.

5. Decision rights

Every material decision in ACTA's lifecycle — initial deployment, retraining, threshold changes, overrides, kill-switch invocation, denial sign-off, expansion, retirement, and vendor changes — has exactly one Accountable role, defined in the Decision RACI. This Framework does not restate that matrix; it establishes that no decision may proceed without a documented Accountable role, full stop.

6. Risk appetite (summary)

The Board has approved a three-tier residual risk appetite for ACTA: ≤8 Within Appetite, 9–14 Accepted with Monitoring (CRO sign-off required), ≥15 Exceeds Appetite (immediate mitigation plan, Board notified within 5 business days). Full detail and current scoring live in the Risk Register.

7. Regulatory context

ACTA operates under Aegis Mutual's SOC 2 Type II and ISO 27001 certifications, each states' unfair claims practices acts, emerging state AI-in-insurance bulletins modeled on the NAIC Model Bulletin on the Use of Artificial Intelligence Systems, and Aegis Mutual's voluntary GDPR-style automated-decision-making safeguards. Full mapping lives in the Compliance Checklist.

8. Amendment

This Framework is reviewed annually by the AI Governance Council and amended only by Council vote with CRO ratification. Material amendments (scope changes, new AI systems added, control-structure changes) require Board Risk Committee notification within one cycle.

Responsible-AI Policy

What ACTA is allowed to do, what it is never allowed to do, and the exact numeric conditions that pull a human into the loop — with a hard stop if that human doesn't act.

Responsible-AI Policy — ACTA (Aegis Auto Claims Triage Assistant)

Acceptable-use rules, prohibited uses, and the human-oversight triggers that govern every claim ACTA touches.
Version1.0
OwnerAI Governance Council
StatusApproved
Date2026-08-29
ClassificationInternal

1. Purpose & scope

This Policy governs how ACTA may be used in claims triage at Aegis Mutual. It applies to every claim ACTA scores, every adjuster and SIU analyst who acts on its recommendations, and every engineer who changes its thresholds or retrains its models.

2. Acceptable use

  • ACTA MAY classify claim severity, produce a SentinelScore-derived fraud-risk indication, recommend a routing decision, and suggest an initial reserve estimate.
  • ACTA MAY auto-approve a claim under Fast-Track only when all three governing conditions hold simultaneously: confidence ≥ 0.90, reserve < $25,000, and fraud score in the LOW band (< 40).
  • ACTA MAY be used only for the claim types and states it has been validated and approved for under RACI Decision #8.

3. Prohibited uses

  • ACTA MUST NOT autonomously deny a claim or communicate a denial without the human sign-off required in Section 4.
  • ACTA MUST NOT use a protected-class attribute, or a known proxy for one (e.g., ZIP code used in place of a legitimate risk factor), as a scoring input without documented, MRM-approved justification and quarterly bias testing.
  • ACTA MUST NOT be deployed to a claim type or state outside its validated scope without a new RACI Decision #8 approval.
  • ACTA MUST NOT be retrained on live production data without Model Risk Management's sign-off under RACI Decision #2.

4. Human-oversight triggers — decision and escalation flow

Every claim ACTA touches passes through this flow. The two shaded exits (Fast-Track auto-approval, and Approve & set reserve) are the only points where a claim proceeds without a human decision already made in that specific case; every other path requires one.

5. Trigger table (summary)

6. Monitoring cadence

Human sign-off compliance is tracked monthly and reported to the AI Governance Council; it has held at or near 100% since the trigger table above was hard-coded into the claim-status state machine rather than left as adjuster guidance.

7. Violations

A confirmed violation of Section 3 (Prohibited Uses) is escalated to the AI Governance Council within one business day. A pattern of violations by a single individual is referred to Human Resources and Compliance & Legal jointly; a systemic violation (e.g., a threshold change that bypassed RACI Decision #3) triggers an immediate Model Risk Management review of every decision made since the bypass occurred.
