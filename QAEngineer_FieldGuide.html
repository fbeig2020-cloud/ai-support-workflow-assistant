QA Engineer Field Guide

The 20% of quality assurance an AI Solution Architect needs in order to direct, evaluate, and approve AI-generated tests and quality evidence — taught concisely, then made concrete with seven real, downloadable deliverables for one worked example.

Who this is for
You are a learner in the Colaberry Enterprise AI Leadership Accelerator becoming an AI Solution Architect. You will rarely write the tests yourself. Your job is to set the quality bar, read the evidence, and decide whether it is trustworthy enough to ship. This guide teaches you to do exactly that — to be the person who can look at an AI-generated test suite and say, with reasons, "this is complete" or "this is theater."

One running example
Every deliverable below is written for a single invented initiative — an auto-insurance AI claims-triage assistant. Read the initiative brief first; everything else refers back to it.

The example initiative — Aegis Auto "Claims Triage Assistant" (ACTA)

Industry: Insurance (personal auto). A believable, mid-size US carrier modernizing its first-notice-of-loss (FNOL) process with an AI assistant.

Aegis Mutual handles roughly 4,200 new auto claims per week. Today, every First Notice of Loss (FNOL) waits in a single queue for a human adjuster to read, classify, and route — average wait 19 hours. Leadership approved ACTA, an AI assistant that reads each FNOL the moment it arrives and produces four things:

  1. a severity classification (Minor / Moderate / Major / Total Loss),
  2. a routing decision (Fast-Track auto-approval vs. Adjuster Review),
  3. a set of fraud-signal flags with a confidence score, and
  4. an initial reserve estimate (the dollar amount the carrier sets aside).

A human adjuster always retains override authority; ACTA never closes a claim on its own. The system is a decision-support tool, not an autonomous decision-maker — a distinction that shapes the entire test strategy.

Why the quality bar is high here
Insurance decisions are regulated, auditable, and money-moving. A wrong "Total Loss" classification, a fraud flag on an honest customer, or a reserve error at scale each carry legal, financial, and reputational blast radius. This is a high blast-radius, partially-irreversible system — so it earns the full test pyramid, not just happy-path checks.

Why QA exists

Software is guilty until proven innocent. Quality assurance is the discipline of producing that proof — systematically, repeatably, and before customers feel the failure. QA is not "clicking around at the end." It is the engineering practice of reducing the uncertainty that a system does what it should, and does not do what it should not.

For AI systems the stakes rise because the runtime is probabilistic. A traditional function returns the same output for the same input. An LLM does not. That is exactly why the Colaberry operating model insists: LLMs are probabilistic; production systems must be deterministic. QA is how we wrap a non-deterministic core in deterministic guarantees — validated inputs, checked outputs, gated side effects, and evidence you can show an auditor.

The architect's one-sentence job
Turn "the demo worked" into "here is the evidence, at a known coverage level, that this system behaves correctly under expected and adverse conditions — and here is what we deliberately did not test."

What QA buys you

  • Confidence to ship — a defensible yes/no, not a vibe.
  • Change safety — regression tests let you modify the model or prompt without silently breaking claim routing.
  • Auditability — traceable evidence linking every requirement to a test result, which regulators and leadership can inspect.
  • Faster iteration — counter-intuitively, good tests speed teams up because failures are caught in seconds, not in production incidents.

These three are constantly confused. The distinction is the difference between policy, project, and procedure.

Mental model
Strategy is the constitution. The plan is the campaign for this election. Cases are the individual ballots. When you review AI output, check that all three exist and agree — AI often produces plausible cases with no strategy behind them.

The testing pyramid

Where should test effort go? The pyramid answers with a shape: many fast, cheap tests at the bottom; few slow, expensive ones at the top. A suite that inverts this — mostly end-to-end — is slow, flaky, and costly to maintain. That inversion is called an "ice-cream cone" and is a process smell.

TierTarget shareFor ACTA, this is…Speed
Unit~70%Severity-score calculator, reserve formula, PII redactor, prompt builder, JSON-shape validator — pure logic, no I/Oms
Integration~20%FNOL intake → classifier → router against a sandbox DB and a mocked LLM; audit-log writesseconds
End-to-end~10%Adjuster console: a real claim flows in, gets triaged, adjuster overrides, audit trail is visibleminutes
Review flag
When an AI generates a test suite, count the tiers. If 60% of the tests spin up a browser, the pyramid is inverted. Ask for the same assertions to be pushed down to unit level.

Functional vs. non-functional testing

Functional tests ask "does it do the right thing?" Non-functional tests ask "does it do the thing well enough?" — fast enough, secure enough, usable enough. Non-functional requirements are where AI-generated suites are weakest, because they require measurable numeric targets that a model will not invent unless told to.

TypeQuestionACTA example (with a number)
FunctionalCorrect behavior?A Total-Loss FNOL is classified "Total Loss" and routed to Adjuster Review, never Fast-Track
PerformanceFast enough?p95 triage latency ≤ 4.0 s; p99 ≤ 8.0 s at 600 claims/day peak
SecuritySafe enough?0 instances of unredacted PII (SSN, full policy #) in logs; prompt-injection in FNOL text cannot change routing
AccessibilityUsable by all?Adjuster console meets WCAG 2.1 AA; keyboard-only override path passes; contrast ≥ 4.5:1
ReliabilityDependable?Triage success rate ≥ 99.5%; on LLM timeout, claim falls back to human queue (no data loss)
The number is the test
"It should be fast" is not testable. "p95 ≤ 4.0 s at peak load" is. Every non-functional requirement you approve must carry a measurable threshold; otherwise it cannot pass or fail, and an AI will happily mark it "verified" without proving anything.

Given / When / Then acceptance tests

Acceptance tests describe behavior in a structured, plain-language form that both a business stakeholder and an automated test can share. The grammar is Given (precondition / context), When (the action / trigger), Then (the observable, checkable outcome).

Worked example (ACTA fraud flag)

Given a FNOL whose loss date is 2 days before the policy's effective date
When ACTA triages the claim
Then a "coverage-gap" fraud signal is raised with confidence ≥ 0.80,
  And the claim is routed to SIU review, never Fast-Track,
  And the reason code FRAUD_COVERAGE_GAP is written to the audit trail.

Good GWT is specific, observable, and single-behavior. If a "Then" needs the word "and" more than twice, it is probably two tests wearing one coat. When you review AI-authored acceptance tests, check that each "Then" asserts something a machine can actually observe — "the model understands the claim" is not checkable; "reason code X is in the audit row" is.

Positive, negative, boundary & idempotency cases

The single most common failure of AI-generated test suites is that they only test the happy path. A complete case set covers four families. If you learn to demand all four, you will catch 80% of weak suites on sight.

FamilyProvesACTA example
POSITIVECorrect behavior on valid, expected inputA moderate collision FNOL is classified "Moderate" and Fast-Tracked
NEGATIVEGraceful handling of invalid / malformed / hostile inputFNOL with a missing policy number is rejected with a 400 and never reaches the classifier; injection text in the description does not alter routing
BOUNDARYCorrect behavior at the edges of a rangeReserve exactly at the $25,000 fast-track ceiling routes to Fast-Track; $25,000.01 routes to Adjuster
IDEMPOTENCYRunning twice produces one effect, not twoThe same FNOL delivered twice (duplicate webhook) creates one claim and one reserve, not two
Idempotency is non-negotiable here
Aegis's FNOL webhook can fire twice on a network retry. If ACTA sets two reserves for one accident, the carrier's books are wrong and the fix is a manual accounting correction. Every side-effecting path (create claim, set reserve, notify SIU) must be gated by an idempotency key — e.g. (fnol_id, event_type) — and there must be a test that fires the same event twice and asserts a single effect.

Traceability & coverage

Traceability is the two-way link between requirements and tests. Coverage is a measure of how much has been exercised. They answer different questions and you need both.

Traceability
Every requirement maps to ≥1 test (no untested requirement), and every test maps back to a requirement (no orphan test). The artifact is the Requirements Traceability Matrix. It is what proves to an auditor that you tested what you promised.
Coverage
A percentage of something exercised — lines, branches, requirements, or risk scenarios. High code coverage with no negative tests is a false comfort. Coverage tells you what you touched; it does not tell you that what you touched is correct.
Two failure shapes to catch
Orphan test: a test that traces to no requirement — either the requirement is missing or the test is scope creep. Untested requirement: a requirement with no test — a hole in the evidence. A clean RTM has neither. This is the fastest single check you can run on an AI's output.

Defect severity vs. priority

These are independent axes and confusing them is a classic tell of an inexperienced (or artificial) tester. Severity = technical impact if it occurs. Priority = business urgency to fix. A defect can be high on one and low on the other.

 Low priorityHigh priority
High severityTotal-loss mis-route, but only on a data pattern that occurs ~once/year — fix it, not tonightReserve set to 10× on any total loss — money-moving, frequent, stop the release
Low severityMisaligned label on an internal-only debug screen — backlogCarrier logo wrong on the customer-facing claim receipt the day of a press event — cosmetic but urgent
"Every defect is Critical" is a red flag
If an AI-generated defect log rates everything Critical, it has no working model of impact. A healthy log shows a distribution across severities and priorities. Demand both axes on every defect, and demand they be justified.
SeverityDefinition (ACTA)
S1 CRITICALMoney moved wrongly, PII leaked, or system down. No workaround.
S2 MAJORCore triage wrong (mis-classification/mis-route) with a manual workaround.
S3 MINORDegraded behavior, cosmetic error with data impact, or slow path.
S4 TRIVIALCosmetic only; no functional or data impact.

Regression, exploratory testing & shift-left

Regression testing

Re-running existing tests to confirm a change did not break something that used to work. For ACTA this matters acutely: a prompt tweak to improve fraud detection could silently change routing on ordinary fender-benders. The regression suite is your safety net for every model, prompt, or rule change.

Exploratory testing

Unscripted, skilled investigation — a human deliberately trying to break the system, learning as they go. It finds what scripted tests miss (weird FNOL phrasing, adversarial photos). It complements automation; it does not replace it.

Shift-left

Move testing earlier — write acceptance tests before the feature, catch defects in design and unit stages where they are 10–100× cheaper to fix than in production. The Colaberry model calls this "test-first": design the failure path before the happy path.

Build–Break–Harden
The repo's execution rhythm: Build the happy path, Break it deliberately (5xx, malformed input, duplicate events, timeouts), then Harden each break with a fix and a test that reproduces it. A feature that has been built but not broken is not shipped.

The architect's review lens

When AI hands you a test suite and a quality report, run this checklist. It is the operational core of your role.

#AskReject if…
1Does every requirement trace to a test, and every test to a requirement?Orphan tests or untested requirements exist
2Are all four case families present per critical behavior?Happy-path only; no negative/boundary/idempotency
3Do non-functional tests carry numeric thresholds?"Should be fast/secure" with no number
4Is the pyramid right-side-up?Mostly E2E; few or no unit tests
5Are side effects idempotent and tested for double-fire?No duplicate-event test on money-moving paths
6Do defects carry both severity and priority, distributed?All "Critical," or defects not logged at all
7Are the tests deterministic (seeds, fixed clock, mocked LLM)?Flaky results; live-model calls in unit tests
8Does the summary end in an explicit ship / no-ship call with evidence?Ends at "looks good"; no decision

1. Purpose & scope

This strategy defines the principles, levels, environments, and gates governing all testing of the Aegis Auto Claims Triage Assistant. It applies to every release of the classifier, router, fraud-signal engine, reserve estimator, and the adjuster console. It is stable across releases; release-specific detail lives in each Test Plan.

2. Quality objectives

  • No FNOL is auto-approved (Fast-Track) that a human policy would have escalated — zero unsafe auto-approvals is the governing constraint.
  • Every money-moving action (reserve set, fast-track pay) is idempotent and auditable.
  • Non-functional targets (latency, PII protection, accessibility) are met with evidence, not assertion.
  • A human adjuster can always override, and the override path is itself tested.

3. Test levels & target distribution

LevelShareWhat it validatesEnvironment
Unit~70%Pure logic: severity scorer, reserve formula, PII redactor, prompt builder, output-schema validatorLocal / CI, no I/O, LLM mocked
Integration~20%Intake→classify→route→persist against sandbox Postgres and a stubbed LLM; audit writesCI sandbox + test DB
End-to-end~10%Adjuster console flows, override, audit visibility (Playwright)Staging, prod-parity

4. Handling the probabilistic core

The LLM is non-deterministic; the system around it must not be. Testing strategy for the AI component:

  • Deterministic wrapper tests: unit-test the deterministic code (validation, routing rules, idempotency) with the LLM mocked to fixed responses.
  • Behavioral evaluation set: a golden set of ~500 labeled historical FNOLs; the model must meet accuracy/precision/recall thresholds on classification and fraud flags (see plan).
  • Contract tests: the LLM output is validated against a strict schema at the boundary; malformed model output fails closed to the human queue.

5. Entry & exit criteria (gates)

GateCriteria
Entry to testRequirements frozen & traced; build deploys to sandbox; unit tests green in CI
Exit / ship100% requirement coverage; 0 open S1; 0 open S2 on money-moving paths; NFR targets met; evidence archived

6. Risk-based prioritization

Test investment scales with blast radius. Reserve calculation and fast-track auto-approval (high blast radius, money-moving) get unit + integration + E2E + adversarial fraud evaluation. Internal debug dashboards (low blast radius) get unit tests only.

7. Environments & data

Local, CI sandbox, and staging run identical Docker compose definitions (dev/prod parity). Test data is de-identified historical FNOLs; no production PII in non-prod. Tests never touch production and never send real customer communications (test-mode flag enforced).

8. Roles

QA Architect owns this strategy and the ship decision. AI (Claude Code) generates cases and evidence under review. Adjuster SMEs validate the golden label set. SIU validates fraud-signal expectations.

FAQ

What's the difference between a test strategy and a test plan?
The strategy is program-wide and stable — it defines how we test in general (levels, environments, gates, risk approach). The plan is release-specific — it defines how we test this release (scope, schedule, resources, the specific risks). Strategy is the constitution; the plan is the campaign. See Strategy vs plan vs cases.
How do I know if an AI-generated test suite is any good?
Run the architect's review lens: check traceability (no orphan tests, no untested requirements), the four case families per critical behavior, numeric thresholds on non-functional tests, a right-side-up pyramid, idempotency tests on money-moving paths, distributed severity+priority on defects, determinism, and an explicit ship/no-ship conclusion.
What's the difference between severity and priority?
Severity is technical impact if the defect occurs; priority is business urgency to fix. They are independent — a rare high-severity bug can be low priority, and a cosmetic bug can be urgent. If every defect is "Critical," the log has no working model of impact. See Severity vs priority.
Why do I need boundary and idempotency tests, not just happy-path?
Because that's where real systems break. DEF-1042 — a $25,000.01 reserve slipping into auto-approval — was caught only by a boundary case. Duplicate webhooks double-setting reserves are caught only by idempotency tests. Happy-path-only suites are the single most common weakness. See the four case families.
What makes a good ship / no-ship decision?
An explicit recommendation backed by evidence: coverage, open defects by severity on which paths, NFR results against targets, and the residual risk. For ACTA the call was conditional ship — release in advisory mode now, hold auto-approval until DEF-1042 is fixed — because the one open defect sits on a money-moving path. See the Test Summary Report.
How should the testing pyramid be shaped?
About 70% unit, 20% integration, 10% end-to-end. E2E tests are slow, flaky, and expensive, so push assertions down to the cheapest level that can prove them. A suite that is mostly E2E is inverted (an "ice-cream cone") and is a process smell. See the testing pyramid.
What is traceability and why does it matter?
Traceability is the two-way link between requirements and tests: every requirement has at least one test, and every test maps back to a requirement. It's what proves to leadership and regulators that you tested what you promised, with no orphan tests and no untested requirements. See the Traceability Matrix.
How do you test a non-deterministic AI component?
Wrap it in deterministic code and test that (validation, routing rules, idempotency) with the model mocked to fixed responses; evaluate the model itself against a labeled golden set with accuracy/recall thresholds; and validate its output against a strict schema at the boundary so malformed output fails closed to a human. See the Test Strategy, section 4.
