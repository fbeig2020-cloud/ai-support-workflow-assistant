Directing, evaluating, and approving AI-generated UX

You are becoming an AI Solution Architect. You will rarely draw the pixels — an AI agent will. Your job is to brief the work, judge whether it is good, and sign it off. This guide gives you the ~20% of user-experience design that lets you do that with authority, then applies every concept to one concrete initiative so the abstractions have somewhere to land.

01 · Foundations

Why UX exists

Software has two audiences: the machine that runs it and the human who uses it. UX is the discipline that keeps the second audience from being an afterthought.

Every product creates an experience whether or not anyone designed it. The only choice is whether that experience is intentional or accidental. UX is the practice of making it intentional: understanding what a real person is trying to accomplish, removing the friction between them and that goal, and proving — with evidence, not opinion — that the friction is actually gone.

Reduce cost

A confused user calls support, abandons the cart, or asks a colleague. Every point of confusion has a price. At Fireside, a customer who can't find a location's pickup window calls the restaurant — the exact cost the app was built to remove.

Reduce risk

Bad UX is not just annoying; it is dangerous. A diner with a shellfish allergy who mis-reads a menu is a lawsuit and a life. Good UX is a control, not a decoration.

Compound trust

Trust is earned in interactions and spent in outages. A reservation that reliably confirms in seconds earns the right to a customer's next celebration.

Why this matters more with AI
An AI agent can generate a plausible screen in seconds. Plausible is not the same as usable. The generator has no memory of the shellfish-allergy diner, no accountability for the abandoned cart, and no intuition for the unhappy path. Your judgment is the control that makes probabilistic output safe to ship. That is the whole reason this guide exists.
02 · Core Knowledge

The 20% you must know

Nine concepts carry most of the weight. Learn to name them, recognize them done well, and demand them when they're missing.

Personas & Jobs-to-be-Done

A persona is a research-grounded archetype of a user — but a useful persona is built on goals and frustrations, not demographics. "45-year-old suburban mother" tells you nothing about how to design. "A parent who is hungry, tired, and wants a reliable meal ready on the drive home" tells you everything.

Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD) reframes the product around the outcome the user "hires" it to achieve: When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]. People don't want a reservation form; they want an effortless celebration. Design for the job, not the feature.

Architect's tell
If a persona could be swapped between two unrelated products without changing a word, it's a demographic sketch, not a design tool. Send it back.

User Journeys

A journey map traces a user's path across an entire task — every stage, and at each stage their actions, thoughts, emotions, and pain points. Its single most valuable feature is that it forces the team to design the unhappy path: the moment the item sells out mid-order, the payment fails, the confirmation never arrives. A journey map that only shows the happy path is marketing, not design.

Information Architecture (IA)

IA is how content and functions are organized, labeled, and connected — the skeleton beneath the visual design. Good IA uses plain-language labels the user already knows ("Reservations," not "Booking Engine") and keeps core tasks shallow. Our rule for Fireside: no core task deeper than three clicks.

Nielsen's 10 Usability Heuristics

Jakob Nielsen's heuristics are the industry's shared checklist for "is this usable?" Memorize them; they are the vocabulary of every design review.

  1. Visibility of system status — always show what's happening.
  2. Match to the real world — speak the user's language.
  3. User control & freedom — easy undo/exit.
  4. Consistency & standards — follow conventions.
  5. Error prevention — stop mistakes before they happen.
  1. Recognition over recall — show options, don't make users remember.
  2. Flexibility & efficiency — accelerators for experts.
  3. Aesthetic & minimalist design — no noise.
  4. Help users recover from errors — plain-language, actionable.
  5. Help & documentation — available when needed.

Accessibility — WCAG 2.1 AA

Accessibility means people with disabilities can use the product — and the legal & ethical floor is WCAG 2.1 Level AA. It rests on four principles (POUR): Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, Robust. The four AA facts you must be able to demand on sight:

Contrast

≥ 4.5:1 for normal text, 3:1 for large text & UI components (SC 1.4.3, 1.4.11).

Keyboard

Everything operable without a mouse; visible focus (SC 2.1.1, 2.4.7).

Labels

Every input and control has a programmatic name (SC 1.3.1, 4.1.2).

Targets

Touch targets ≥ 24×24 px (SC 2.5.8, AA).

Visual & Interaction Hierarchy

Hierarchy is how a design answers "where do I look, and what do I do?" without words. Size, weight, color, and spacing rank elements; the single most important action on any screen should be visually loudest. On the Fireside cart, "Place Order" is the only Cherry-Red button on the screen — that scarcity is what makes it read as the action.

Design Systems & Tokens

A design system is the reusable kit — components, patterns, and rules — that keeps a product consistent. Its atoms are design tokens: named, semantic variables like color.action.primary instead of a raw #FB2832 sprinkled across files. Tokens are what let you change the whole product's spacing or theme in one place, and what make dark mode a data change rather than a rewrite.

Usability Testing

The only evidence that beats opinion. You give real users real tasks and watch where they struggle. Five users typically surface ~85% of usability problems. The output is not "they liked it" — it's a ranked list of observed failures with severity. Everything else in UX is a hypothesis until testing confirms it.

03 · Judgment

Good vs bad design

Same feature — Fireside's pickup checkout — done two ways. Train your eye on the difference.

Good

  • Estimated pickup time shown before payment, updated live.
  • Saved card offered; guest checkout allowed — no forced account.
  • Sold-out item removes itself with a clear message and a suggested swap.
  • One primary action per screen; errors appear next to the field, in plain language.
  • Works with keyboard only; focus is always visible; contrast passes AA.
  • Order confirmation arrives instantly, with a tracking link.

Bad

  • Pickup time revealed only after paying — a nasty surprise.
  • Account creation forced before checkout; cart lost on signup.
  • Sold-out item fails silently at payment with "Error 402."
  • Three competing buttons; a red generic "An error occurred" banner.
  • Card field unreachable by keyboard; focus invisible; grey-on-grey text.
  • "Confirmation" is a spinner that never resolves.
The pattern
Good design is mostly the absence of avoidable friction and the presence of a designed failure path. Bad design almost always fails the same handful of heuristics: hidden status, forced recall, silent errors, no undo.

The SUS trap
SUS is 0–100 but it is not a percentage. A score of 68 is the median — average, not a "68% pass." Anything below ~68 is below average; 80+ is the top decile. Never let an AI's report describe SUS as a percent.
05 · The Job

The architect's review lens

When an AI hands you UX work, run it through this lens before you approve. Ten questions, in order.

  1. Whose job is this serving? Point to the persona and the JTBD. If it can't, it's decoration.
  2. Where's the unhappy path? Show me empty, loading, error, and success states — not just the happy screen.
  3. Is the hierarchy honest? Is the loudest element actually the most important action?
  4. Three clicks? Trace the core task. Count the clicks. Enforce the ceiling.
  5. Which heuristics does it violate? Name them. Vague "feels off" is not a review.
  6. Does it pass AA? Contrast numbers, keyboard operability, focus, labels, target size. Demand the evidence.
  7. Are tokens semantic? Raw hex scattered in components is technical debt. Send it back.
  8. Is it consistent? Same action, same look, everywhere.
  9. What does the data say? Is there a test plan and a baseline, or just vibes?
  10. Would I sign my name to it? The sign-off block exists because approval is accountability.
Non-negotiable
Never approve UX where the failure states are undefined or the accessibility evidence is missing. Those two gaps are where AI-generated design most reliably fails, and where the cost of shipping the failure is highest.

Personas are grounded in goals and frustrations observed across 22 customer interviews and 6 host-stand shadowing sessions. Demographics are context, never the design driver. Three primary personas and one internal (staff) persona shape every screen.

Primary · P1

Marcus — "The Weeknight Parent"

Context: Leaving work at 6pm, two hungry kids, 20-minute drive home. Orders 3–4× a week from the same two restaurants.

Goals: Reorder a known-good family meal in under two minutes; know the exact time it'll be ready; pay without re-typing a card.

Frustrations: Unclear or wrong pickup times; forced account creation; having to rebuild the same order every time; discovering an item is sold out only at the counter.

JTBD: "When I'm leaving work hungry, I want to reorder a meal my family already likes in under two minutes, so I can pick it up ready on my drive home."

Primary · P2

Dana — "The Occasion Planner"

Context: Books tables for birthdays, anniversaries, and work dinners weeks ahead. Coordinates 6–10 guests.

Goals: Confirm a table is actually held; communicate dietary notes and the occasion in advance; feel confident nothing will fall through.

Frustrations: Reservations that never send a confirmation; phone tag with the host stand; no way to note "wheelchair access" or "birthday" ahead of time.

JTBD: "When I'm planning a celebration, I want to secure a table and share the details in advance, so the evening feels effortless and I look thoughtful."

Primary · P3

Priya — "The Allergy-Aware Diner"

Context: Severe shellfish allergy. Eats out often but only where she can verify safety.

Goals: Filter the menu to safe dishes; trust the allergen information; flag her allergy on the order so the kitchen sees it.

Frustrations: Allergen info buried or absent; ingredients hidden behind "ask your server"; no way to attach a note the kitchen actually reads.

JTBD: "When I have a severe allergy, I want to confirm a dish is safe before I order, so I can eat out without anxiety."

Secondary · S1

Theo — "The Host on Shift"

Context: Runs the host stand during dinner rush. Manages the floor, walk-ins, and now the digital reservation queue on a tablet.

Goals: See incoming reservations at a glance; seat efficiently; not get double-booked by the app and the phone.

Frustrations: Tools that assume a quiet desk and two free hands; tiny tap targets during a rush; no clear "held vs seated" status.

JTBD: "When the floor is slammed, I want to see and manage the table queue in one glance, so I never double-seat or lose a booking."

Moderator script (excerpt)

Intro
"Thanks for joining. We're testing the app, not you — there are no wrong answers. Please think aloud: say what you're looking at, what you expect, and anything that surprises you. I'll stay quiet and won't help unless you're truly stuck, because we need to see how the app works on its own."
  1. Warm-up: "How do you usually order takeout or book a table today?"
  2. Present T1. Observe silently. Log: completed? unaided? time? errors? emotion?
  3. Post-task question set (below) after each task.
  4. Repeat for T2–T4.
  5. Administer the 10-item SUS. Then wrap-up questions.

Post-task questions (after every task)

  • Ease (1–7): "How easy or difficult was that task?" (Single Ease Question)
  • Confidence: "How confident are you the outcome is correct?"
  • Expectation: "Did anything happen you didn't expect?"
  • Improvement: "If you could change one thing about that, what would it be?"

Wrap-up & analysis

Score SUS (0–100). Compile a ranked issue list with severity (reuse the D5 scale). A finding seen by ≥2 of 6 participants is prioritized. Report format: metric dashboard + top-5 issues + video clips. Re-test after fixes to confirm the severity-4 items are resolved (idempotent process: same tasks, same metrics, compare deltas).

The opportunity

Fireside Table Group loses margin to third-party delivery apps (22–30%) and turns away dinner-rush orders when phone lines jam. Fireside Direct — a first-party ordering & reservations PWA with an AI menu concierge — recaptures that demand on owned rails.

What the UX program produced

  • 3 primary personas grounded in goals, not demographics (D1).
  • An end-to-end journey map that designs the two failure moments — sold-out swap and declined-card retry — that otherwise send customers back to the phone (D2).
  • A three-click information architecture with plain-language labels (D3).
  • Wireframes specified for every state — empty, loading, error, success (D4).
  • A heuristic evaluation that surfaced 11 findings, 2 of them release-blocking (D5).
  • A WCAG 2.1 AA audit with real contrast numbers and two must-fix conformance gaps (D6).
  • A semantic design-token system that makes theming and dark mode a data change (D7).
  • A usability test plan to prove the redesign before it ships (D8).
