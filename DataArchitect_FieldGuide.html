Data Architect Field Guide

The ~20% of data architecture an AI Solution Architect must master to direct, evaluate, and approve what an AI drafts — data models, pipelines, schemas, and governance policy. You are not building this warehouse yourself. You are the reviewer who has to catch it when the model is wrong before it ships to production and gets expensive to fix.

The Example Initiative

Aegis Data Foundation — Aegis Mutual Insurance

Aegis Mutual is a fictional mid-size auto insurer used across this curriculum track — ACTA, the Aegis Auto Claims Triage Assistant, is the claims-side AI capability covered in the QA, UX Design, and Integration Engineer field guides. This guide covers the data platform underneath all of it: the Aegis Data Foundation (ADF), a governed enterprise data warehouse that unifies policy data (from the Policy Admin System, PAS), claims data (from ACTA's operational database), payment data (from ClaimPay), and third-party risk signals (DocScan document extraction, SentinelScore fraud scoring — both already landed inside ACTA's claim record) into one queryable, trustworthy source for actuarial reporting, financial close, claims operations analytics, and state regulatory filings.

Today, none of that reporting is governed. PAS, ACTA, and ClaimPay each define "open claim," "policy tenure," and "earned premium" slightly differently, because each system was built for its own operational purpose, not for cross-system reporting. Every quarter, actuarial analysts manually reconcile spreadsheets pulled from all three systems to produce the loss-ratio numbers Aegis has to file with state insurance regulators — a process that takes roughly nine business days and has produced at least one restated figure in each of the last four filing cycles, because a "closed" claim in ACTA does not always mean the payment in ClaimPay has actually cleared.

Foundations

Conceptual, Logical & Physical Models

Every data model an AI drafts should be reviewable at three distinct altitudes, and conflating them is the single most common way a data model goes wrong. The conceptual model is business language only — entities like Policy, Claim, and Payment, and the relationships between them, with no mention of columns, data types, or which database engine will hold them. It is the model you review with actuaries and claims leadership, and if they cannot read it, it has failed its purpose. The logical model attributes those entities — every field named, typed at a business level (text, date, money, not yet varchar(40) or numeric(12,2)), primary and foreign keys identified, still independent of any specific platform. The physical model is the model as it will actually run: real column types for a real engine, indexes, partitioning, constraints — the thing captured as executable DDL.

The failure mode to watch for is an AI collapsing all three into one physical-only artifact and calling it "the data model." A stakeholder review of raw DDL is not a design review — nobody outside data engineering can evaluate whether policy_id BIGINT REFERENCES policy(id) reflects the business correctly. Insist on seeing the conceptual and logical layers first, get agreement from the business owners on those, and only then let the physical model follow. See DOC 01 for all three layers built out for the Aegis Data Foundation.

What to watch for when an AI drafts this A "data model" document that opens with CREATE TABLE statements and never states, in plain language, what a Policy or a Claim is to the business has skipped the two layers that actually need your review. Send it back.

Entities, Relationships & ERD Cardinality

An entity is a real, distinguishable thing the business tracks with a stable identity over time — a Policy, a Vehicle, a Claim — not an attribute of another entity wearing a table's clothing. A relationship states how two entities associate, and its cardinality — how many of one can relate to how many of the other — is the single fact about a relationship that is most often drawn wrong, and most consequential when it is. Crow's-foot notation reads cardinality at each end of the connecting line: a single tick means "exactly one," two ticks mean "one and mandatory," a circle means "zero (optional)," and a three-pronged crow's foot means "many."

Reviewing cardinality is not a formality — it is where silent data-loss bugs get built in. If an AI models Policy-to-Vehicle as one-to-one when Aegis routinely insures multiple vehicles on one policy, every join downstream under-counts vehicles and every per-vehicle premium calculation is wrong, and no unit test written against the wrong model will ever catch it, because the test was written against the same wrong assumption. Ask, for every relationship: what is the real minimum and maximum on each side, backed by an actual example from the business, not an assumption.

Normalization vs. Denormalization

Normalization is the discipline of storing each fact exactly once, organized so that update anomalies are structurally impossible: a policyholder's address lives in one row, referenced everywhere it is needed, so changing it once changes it everywhere. Third normal form (3NF) — no repeating groups, every non-key attribute depends on the whole key, and on nothing but the key — is the right default for operational, write-heavy systems like PAS or ACTA's claim database, where consistency on every write matters more than read speed on any single query.

Denormalization deliberately reintroduces redundancy to make reads fast and simple, and it is the right default for an analytical warehouse, where data is loaded in controlled batches (so update anomalies are managed by the pipeline, not by chance) and read thousands of times by people who should never have to write a six-table join to find a claim's policy number. The dimensional star schema in DOC 01 is denormalization applied deliberately: DimCustomer repeats the customer's state and agency name on every row so that an actuarial analyst's query never needs a join to filter by state.

The review question Not "is this normalized?" but "is the normalization level right for how this table is written and read?" A highly normalized warehouse fact table just means your BI tool's SQL becomes an unreadable wall of joins; a denormalized OLTP table just means your application has silently reintroduced the update-anomaly bugs 3NF exists to prevent.

OLTP vs. OLAP, Warehouse vs. Lake

OLTP (online transaction processing) systems — PAS, ACTA's operational database, ClaimPay's ledger — are optimized for many small, fast, concurrent writes: one policy issued, one claim status changed, one payment posted. OLAP (online analytical processing) systems — the Aegis Data Foundation warehouse — are optimized for the opposite access pattern: relatively few large queries that scan and aggregate millions of rows, run by analysts and BI tools, not by the application serving a live transaction. Trying to run actuarial trend analysis directly against PAS is why quarter-close reporting hurts today — every heavy analytical query competes with the live traffic PAS was built to serve, and PAS's normalized, 20-year-old schema was never designed to answer "loss ratio by state by quarter" in one query.

A data warehouse stores structured, modeled, schema-on-write data — the star schema in DOC 01 — built for known, recurring reporting questions. A data lake stores raw or lightly-processed data of any shape — schema-on-read — built for exploratory analysis and use cases nobody has defined yet. The Aegis Data Foundation uses both: a raw zone (lake-like, append-only, holds exactly what PAS, ACTA, and ClaimPay sent, unmodified) feeding a curated warehouse zone (the governed star schema everyone actually queries). See DOC 04 for how data moves between them.

ETL vs. ELT & Pipeline Design

ETL (extract, transform, load) transforms data in flight, before it lands in the target — the transformation logic lives in a separate processing layer, and only clean, modeled data ever reaches the warehouse. ELT (extract, load, transform) loads raw data first and transforms it afterward, using the warehouse engine's own compute — this is the dominant modern pattern because cloud warehouses are cheap to scale and it keeps a complete, unmodified copy of source data for reprocessing or audit. The Aegis Data Foundation is ELT: PAS, ACTA, and ClaimPay data lands raw in the staging zone first, then SQL-based transformations build the conformed dimensions and facts — so if a transformation bug is discovered six months later, it can be fixed and rerun against the preserved raw history rather than needing the source systems to resend data they may no longer have.

Whichever pattern an AI proposes, the non-negotiable property is idempotency: running the same pipeline stage twice on the same input must produce the same end state, never duplicate rows or double-counted amounts. This is what makes a pipeline safe to retry after a failure instead of a source of silent data corruption. See DOC 04 for the exact idempotency mechanism used at every stage of the Aegis Data Foundation pipeline.

The single most important pipeline question "What happens if this exact batch runs twice?" If the honest answer is "it would double the numbers," the pipeline is not done, regardless of how clean the happy-path transformation logic looks.

Data Quality & Lineage

Data quality rules fall into four families, and a complete quality plan needs all four, not just the easy ones: validity (does the value conform to its expected format or range — a VIN is 17 characters, a state code is a real state), completeness (is required data present — every claim has a loss date), uniqueness (does a key actually identify one row — one policy number, one row), and consistency (do related facts agree with each other — a claim's paid amount never exceeds its incurred amount). Quality rules that only check validity are the easiest to write and the least likely to catch the bug that actually costs money; a paid-amount-exceeds-incurred-amount consistency violation is a financial-reporting error, not a formatting nitpick.

Lineage is the traceable path from a number in a report back to the source system and column it came from, through every transformation in between. Without published lineage, "why does this number look wrong?" turns into a multi-day archaeology project across three systems and a pipeline nobody fully remembers writing; with it, it is a lookup. Every reported KPI in a governed warehouse should have a one-hop answer to "where did this come from," which is precisely what DOC 08 exists to make possible for the Aegis Data Foundation.

Master Data & the Data Dictionary

Master data is the small set of core business entities — customers, vehicles, products, employees — that many systems reference and that must have one agreed-upon, trustworthy version: the "golden record." When PAS spells a policyholder's name one way and ACTA's claimant intake spells it another, master data management is the discipline that decides which system wins, under what rule, and publishes the resolved golden record for everyone downstream to consume — rather than every consuming system inventing its own guess. Reference data is the smaller, more stable cousin: shared code sets like claim-status values or coverage-type abbreviations that must mean the same thing everywhere they appear.

The data dictionary is the contract that makes any of this reviewable: every field, in every table that matters, with its name, type, business meaning, source system, whether it can be null, and whether it carries personally identifiable information. An AI-drafted schema without an accompanying data dictionary is not reviewable — you would be approving column names you cannot verify mean what they claim to mean. See DOC 02 for the full Aegis Data Foundation dictionary, and DOC 09 for the golden-record rules and shared code sets.

Governance, PII & Retention

Data governance is the set of decisions about who may access what data, for what purpose, for how long, and who is accountable when something goes wrong — decisions that must be made explicitly, in writing, before a warehouse holding sensitive data ships, not discovered during an audit. PII (personally identifiable information) must be classified field by field, not table by table — a Claim table might hold a claimant's driver's license number in one column and a purely mechanical fault description in the next, and only one of those two needs masking, restricted access, and a retention clock.

Retention in an insurance context is unusually strict in both directions: records must be kept long enough to satisfy state regulatory recordkeeping requirements (commonly multiple years past a claim's closure), and a data-subject deletion request cannot simply erase a financial or claims record that the company is legally obligated to retain — the correct response is typically to restrict access and flag the record, not delete it outright. An AI that proposes a blanket "delete on request" policy for a claims warehouse has not accounted for this, and it is exactly the kind of governance gap a Solution Architect is responsible for catching. See DOC 06 for the full PII classification and retention policy.

What to watch for when an AI drafts this A governance document that treats every field the same way — all PII or none of it, one retention period for the whole warehouse — has not actually done the classification work. Insist on a field-by-field table, not a paragraph of good intentions.

Schema Evolution

Source systems change — PAS adds a new coverage type, ACTA starts capturing a new fraud-score dimension, a state regulator mandates a new reportable field — and a warehouse schema has to absorb that change without breaking every report and pipeline job that already depends on it. The disciplined pattern is additive-first: new columns are nullable and appended, never inserted into the middle of a table or silently repurposed from an old column's meaning; a genuinely breaking change (a column's type or meaning actually changes) ships as a new versioned table or view alongside the old one, with a published migration window, never as a silent in-place rewrite.

Slowly Changing Dimensions (SCD) are the specific technique for a very common evolution case: a dimension's own attributes change over time and the warehouse needs to decide whether to remember the history. SCD Type 1 overwrites the old value (correct for fixing a typo). SCD Type 2 keeps every version as a new row with effective-dated validity ranges (correct for "what was this customer's address on the day of loss," which actuarial and claims analysis genuinely need). Getting this choice wrong on DimPolicy or DimCustomer quietly destroys history the business will eventually need. See DOC 04 for where SCD Type 2 is applied in the Aegis Data Foundation.

Good vs. Bad Design, KPIs & the Review Lens

A good data model has one entity per real business thing with a stable key, every relationship's cardinality backed by a real example, normalization applied where things are written and denormalization applied only where things are read, every field traceable to a source, and no "just in case" columns nobody can define. A bad data model shows itself in a small number of repeating patterns: the one-wide-table (every fact about the business jammed into a single table because joins felt like extra work), orphan columns (fields with no documented meaning, source, or owner — usually the first casualty when an AI is asked to move fast), and silent grain mismatches (a fact table that claims to be "one row per claim" but is actually one row per claim-status-change, so every naive COUNT(*) overcounts).

The review lens that catches most of this in practice is simple and repeatable: pick three or four real KPIs the business actually reports — for Aegis, loss ratio, claim cycle time, fraud flag rate, written premium — and trace each one, by hand, from the report back through the model to a named source column. If you cannot complete that trace in a few minutes, the model is not done, no matter how clean the diagram looks. This is the same exercise DOC 08 performs formally for every reported field in the Aegis Data Foundation.

The five-minute review test Open the ERD. Pick any two connected entities. Ask the AI (or yourself) to state the cardinality between them and name one real, concrete example of each side. If that takes more than a minute or produces a vague answer, the relationship was drawn, not designed.

6. Physical / Analytical Model — Star Schema

The warehouse implements the conceptual model above as a dimensional star schema: two fact tables at the grain of a claim and a payment transaction, surrounded by conformed dimensions shared across both. This shape is deliberately denormalized (see Normalization vs. Denormalization) — an actuarial analyst filtering by state and coverage type should never need to write a join beyond the fact table and its dimensions.

Dashed red lines: FactPayment shares DimDate with FactClaim and carries claim_key as a degenerate dimension back to the claim — this two-fact shape is a fact constellation, the schema-evolution step described in Schema Evolution.

1. Pattern: ELT, Not ETL

The Aegis Data Foundation loads raw source data first and transforms it afterward inside the warehouse engine (see ETL vs. ELT). This preserves a complete, unmodified copy of every batch ever received — if a transformation bug is found months later, it is fixed and rerun against preserved history rather than requiring PAS, a 20-year-old system with no long-term extract archive of its own, to resend data it may no longer have.

2. Pipeline Flow

Solid lines: the pass path. Dashed red: a row that fails a Data Quality Rule (DOC 05) is diverted to the dead-letter store with full context, never silently dropped and never allowed to corrupt a conformed table.

3. Stage Definitions

StageWhat happensRe-run behavior
ExtractSource-specific pull: PAS nightly full snapshot (flat file, no native CDC), ACTA logical-replication change stream, ClaimPay daily settlement file plus real-time webhook eventsEach extract is tagged with a batch ID; re-running an extract for an already-processed window overwrites that window's raw file, it never appends a duplicate
Land (Raw Zone)Raw payload stored exactly as received, partitioned by source and batch date, schema-on-readAppend-only by batch ID; re-landing the same batch ID is a no-op (checked before write)
StageTyped, deduplicated, structurally validated staging tables — one per source tableBuilt with TRUNCATE + INSERT per batch, or MERGE keyed on natural key + batch id — never a blind append
Quality GateEvery Data Quality Rule in DOC 05 evaluated; passing rows proceed, failing rows and their failed rule IDs are written to the dead-letter storeDeterministic given the same staged input — a re-run produces the same pass/fail split
Transform / ConformBuilds SCD Type 2 dimensions (dim_customer, dim_policy) and merges fact tables at their declared grainMERGE / INSERT ... ON CONFLICT keyed on natural key + effective date; safe to rerun the same batch any number of times
PublishConformed star schema exposed to BI tools, actuarial notebooks, and the regulatory filing extractViews/materialized views refreshed after Transform completes and passes a row-count sanity check

4. Idempotency Mechanism per Source

Source operationIdempotency keyMechanism
PAS nightly policy/vehicle/coverage extract(extract_batch_id, policy_number)MERGE into dim_policy keyed on policy_number; a new SCD2 row is opened only when a tracked attribute actually differs from the current version
ACTA claim change-data-capture eventchange_event_idDedup table checked before apply; UPSERT into staging keyed on (claim_number, updated_at)
ClaimPay daily settlement file(settlement_batch_id, transaction_id)INSERT ... ON CONFLICT (claimpay_transaction_id) DO NOTHING into fact_payment staging
ClaimPay real-time payment webhook (mirrors the integration-layer design)Idempotency-Key header / event_idDedup table keyed on event_id, checked before the row is queued for the next Stage run
Full pipeline replay for a historical batch datebatch_dateReplaying a batch date re-executes every stage for that date only; conformed tables are restated via MERGE, never truncated wholesale
The single most important pipeline question "What happens if this exact batch runs twice?" For every row above, the answer is the same end state with zero duplicate rows and zero double-counted amounts — verified by a re-run test in CI before this pipeline is allowed to ship.

5. Late-Arriving Data

ClaimPay settlement confirmations can arrive up to 3 business days after a payment is initiated. The pipeline keeps a rolling 5-business-day reprocessing window: any fact table row inside that window is eligible for restatement via MERGE when a late confirmation lands, and every restatement is logged with the prior and new values for audit. Data older than the window is treated as final and requires an explicit, logged manual correction rather than automatic restatement.

6. Failure-First Design

QuestionAnswer
What happens if this fails?The failing stage halts for that batch only; upstream stages already committed are untouched, and downstream stages simply see no new data until the failure is resolved
Will it retry, and how?Yes — capped exponential backoff, 3 attempts (1 min, 5 min, 15 min), then the batch is marked failed and does not silently retry forever
Recovery path if retries are exhausted?The batch lands in a failed-batch queue with full error context; Data Engineering is paged, and the batch is manually re-triggered once the root cause is fixed — never auto-skipped
What failure modes are explicitly handled vs. not?Handled: source unavailable, malformed row, duplicate delivery, schema drift (new nullable column). Not handled: a source system silently returning wrong-but-valid-looking data — this is caught only by the consistency rules in DOC 05, not by the pipeline itself

4. Deletion Requests & the Right-to-Erasure Boundary

A data-subject deletion request against a claim, policy, or payment record inside its active retention window is not fulfilled by deletion — insurance recordkeeping obligations legally require Aegis to retain these records for the periods above. The correct response is to log the request, verify identity, and restrict standing access to the record to the Compliance/DPO role only, with a flag recorded on the record noting the request and its disposition. Only fields with no retention basis attached to a claim, policy, or payment (there are none in this warehouse's current scope) would be eligible for immediate hard deletion. Once a record's retention window has genuinely expired, it is purged by a scheduled, logged job — not left indefinitely "just in case."

What to watch for when an AI drafts this A governance document that proposes "delete on request" for every table has not accounted for insurance recordkeeping law. This is exactly the kind of governance gap a Solution Architect must catch before a policy like that ships and creates a legal exposure the opposite of what it intended to solve.

1. Golden Record — Customer (Policyholder)

PAS is the system of record for policyholder identity — legal name, date of birth, mailing address, email, and phone all survive from PAS, never from ACTA, because PAS reflects the contractual policy record Aegis is legally bound to. When ACTA's claim intake captures a claimant who is also the policyholder, that claimant record is linked to the existing policyholder_key for analytics but never allowed to overwrite PAS-sourced fields — ACTA is authoritative for claim-context data only.

Match strategyRule
Primary matchpas_party_id — deterministic, always used when present
Fallback match (ACTA claimant to PAS policyholder)Fuzzy match on name + date of birth + ZIP code, used only to link a claimant to an existing golden record for reporting — never to create or overwrite a golden record field
Field-level survivorshipPAS wins for identity/contact fields; ACTA wins only for claim-specific attributes that have no PAS equivalent
Non-policyholder claimantsKept in the source-aligned claim-party record, never merged into the customer golden record — a claimant is not automatically a customer

2. Golden Record — Vehicle

The VIN is the natural, immutable key for a physical vehicle and never changes across policy periods, even if the vehicle is sold and re-insured under a different policy. dim_vehicle attributes (make, model, model year) are sourced from PAS at binding and are not expected to change for a given VIN; if a correction is needed, it is applied in place (SCD Type 1) because these are immutable physical facts about the vehicle, not attributes with a meaningful history to preserve.
